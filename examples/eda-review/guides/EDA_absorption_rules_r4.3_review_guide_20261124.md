# Absorption EDA r4.3 · Study review guide

## Main question

**When did parent drug first reach the circulation, how much of the dose reached it, and which absorption models could explain the observed concentrations?**

Review one study and species at a time. After an extravascular dose, such as an oral or subcutaneous dose, concentration depends on both systemic input and disposition. A comparable intravenous profile helps distinguish the two. Record the drug present before dosing, the measured analyte and the sampling design.

## Candidate models and what they mean

After a single dose, three quantities describe systemic input. The eventual amount of parent drug reaching the circulation is $FD$, where $D$ is the administered dose and $F$ is systemic bioavailability. The fraction of that amount that has arrived by time $t$ is $G(t)$. The rate of entry is $FD\,dG(t)/dt$, in amount per unit time. The measured concentration also depends on distribution and clearance.

**Input functions for model comparison.** Start with immediate first-order absorption. Move to an alternative when the observations or the formulation give a reason to compare it. In the formulas, $t$ is time since input starts unless a lag is shown. The third column says whether the concentration/time check (question 4, Figure 2) and the within-animal trough check (question 2, Figure 1b) can challenge each pattern. Both predictions depend on the stated disposition, baseline and measurement assumptions.

| Input pattern | Cumulative fraction or mechanism | What would distinguish it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Immediate first-order** | $G(t)=1-\exp(-k_a t)$. A constant fraction of the amount still available enters per unit time. | The rate falls from dosing. Under the stated disposition, concentration/time cannot rise and the profile has at most one peak. If the first draw is already past the rise, very fast uptake may be indistinguishable from an instantaneous-input approximation. Report the unresolved time scale. |
| **Finite zero-order** | Drug enters at a constant rate for duration $T$. The cumulative fraction is $t/T$ up to $T$, then 1. | This input meets both predictions above. Samples around the proposed end of input or independent delivery records can distinguish it from first-order absorption. |
| **Fixed lag before a chosen input** | For lag $L$, entry is zero through $L$. Thereafter evaluate the chosen input function at time since dosing minus $L$. | A fixed lag falls outside the immediate-input check. Shifting a single-peaked response in time preserves its shape. An early below-limit sample alone cannot establish a lag. |
| **Serial transit, Erlang or gamma input** | In a chain of $n$ equal-rate first-order waiting stages with no extra final step, the arrival-time density is Erlang. A gamma density allows positive, noninteger shape $a$, with rate proportional to $t^{a-1}\exp(-t/\theta)$ for scale $\theta>0$. State whether there is a separate final absorption step. | For the gamma input itself, $a=1$ gives first-order input, $a>1$ gives a gradual rise, and $0<a<1$ gives a rate that falls from dosing. The density is log-concave for $a\ge1$. A hard lag and a gradual delay are best compared with closely spaced early samples. |
| **Zero-order release into a depot with first-order uptake** | For release duration $T$, $G(t)=[t-(1-\exp(-k_a t))/k_a]/T$ through $T$. Thereafter $G(t)=1-(1-\exp(-k_aT))\exp[-k_a(t-T)]/(k_aT)$. Drug can enter the circulation while release is still underway. | The input rate rises from zero, then falls. These two processes in series give a log-concave rate for a single release, so the single-peak prediction applies under the stated disposition. |
| **Parallel inputs with a common start** | Split the eventual systemic input between two first-order rates or between finite zero-order and first-order input. The cumulative fraction is the weighted sum of the two functions, with positive weights that sum to 1. | When both components start at dosing, the total rate is non-increasing. Dense early and late samples help distinguish fast and slow components. Applying the trough test to a particular mixture requires a single-peak argument for that mixture. |
| **Staggered or pulsed inputs** | A fraction $w$ follows $G_1(t)$ and the rest follows $G_2(t-L)$ after a second onset at $L$, with zero contribution from that path beforehand. Weighted pairs of Weibull or inverse Gaussian functions can also describe waves with different arrival times. | Look for a reproducible trough and second rise in the same animal. Compare a second release with intermittent gastric emptying and recirculation. Early burst plus prolonged release is a candidate for depot formulations. Check the formulation and sample near both rises. |
| **Sequential input from two dose fractions** | For example, a fraction $w$ enters at a constant rate up to $T$. First-order absorption of the remaining fraction starts at $T$. The two fractions enter in successive periods. | Sample on both sides of $T$. Compare the rate just before the switch, $w/T$, with the starting rate of the second fraction, $(1-w)k_a$, on the same fractional basis. An upward jump falls outside the non-increasing-input assumption. In the release-to-depot model above, uptake starts during release. |
| **Weibull** | $G(t)=1-\exp[-(t/\tau)^\beta]$. At $t=\tau$, 63.2% of eventual input has entered. With $\beta=1$ this is first-order input with $k_a=1/\tau$. | With no lag, $0<\beta\le1$ gives non-increasing input. The single-peak proof covers $\beta\ge1$ under the stated disposition. An initially rising rate at $\beta>1$ can resemble transit input. |
| **Inverse Gaussian** | With mean absorption time $m$, squared coefficient of variation $v$, $a=\sqrt{m/(vt)}$ and standard normal cumulative distribution $\Phi$, $G(t)=\Phi[a(t/m-1)]+\exp(2/v)\Phi[-a(t/m+1)]$ for $t>0$. | The input rate rises, then falls, and is log-concave only through $2m/(3v)$. The single-peak prediction holds with one-compartment disposition under the stated initial-state and observation assumptions. A multicompartment model requires a separate argument. |
| **Distributed release followed by uptake** | A release-time density, for example inverse Gaussian, feeds a depot that empties by first-order absorption. The systemic input rate is the convolution of release and uptake. | Compare a release-only model with release plus uptake when dissolution or formulation information and an independent disposition profile can separate the two time scales. A release curve measured in vitro needs evidence linking it to release in vivo. |
| **Other smooth arrival-time distributions** | As empirical sensitivity models, a lognormal input has $G(t)=\Phi[\ln(t/t_{50})/\sigma]$ for $t>0$. A log-logistic (Hill) cumulative input has $G(t)=(t/t_{50})^h/[1+(t/t_{50})^h]$. Here $t_{50}$ is the median arrival time and the shape parameters are positive. | Compare these forms when tail extrapolation or the timing of input matters and the study samples the rise and tail. For the log-logistic model, mean arrival time is finite only for $h>1$. Check this before reporting mean absorption time. |
| **Finite absorption window** | Absorption operates between specified entry and exit times. For first-order uptake, the unabsorbed depot remaining at the cutoff can be carried forward as unabsorbed drug. Define $G(t)$ relative to the amount that ultimately enters. | Compare sustained slow uptake with uptake that ends when drug leaves an absorptive region. State what constrains the cutoff, such as regional delivery, transit measurements or formulation data. The last quantifiable plasma sample cannot locate it. |
| **Time-dependent uptake rate** | A single depot empties at $k_a(t)A(t)$, where $A(t)$ is the amount available. Specify how the coefficient changes, for example across two periods or through a smooth transition. | Compare with a fixed lag or two input episodes when changes recur at similar times. Apply a non-increasing-input argument to the **total rate** $k_a(t)A(t)$ over the full window. Changes in the coefficient alone leave the direction of the total rate open. |
| **Capacity-limited uptake, with optional passive uptake** | One example of an uptake rate is $V_{\max}C_s/(K_m+C_s)+CL_p C_s$, where $C_s$ is concentration at the uptake site, $V_{\max}$ is amount/time, $K_m$ is a concentration and $CL_p$ is a passive uptake clearance. Account for subsequent first-pass loss where applicable. | Compare normalized profiles at several doses and use intravenous data to check clearance. If site concentration only falls, both uptake terms fall within that dose even when uptake is nonlinear across doses. Transit loss can lower ultimate extent. Transport or permeability data help identify this mechanism. |
| **Flexible input estimated from the data** | Estimate a nonnegative piecewise-constant, piecewise-linear or spline input rate. Constrain its integrated amount and state the time grid or smoothing choice. | With sufficiently dense observations and an independent disposition reference, compare a standard input with the reconstructed one. Check whether a second pulse survives alternate grids, smoothing and plausible disposition estimates. Use this analysis in Modeling. |

The coverage of a shape check follows from the **complete** model. A change in onset, a second input, local loss or a different disposition can change the prediction. The table includes modeling options beyond the machine-readable r4.3 list. For each additional option, name the eligible check or explain why the profile supports only a descriptive comparison. The formal exclusion criteria and uncertainty requirements are given with Figures 1 and 2.

**Flip-flop kinetics.** The same input function can yield an absorption-controlled or a disposition-controlled terminal decline. In the one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination, the slope of log concentration eventually approaches $-k_a$ when absorption is slower than elimination ($k_a<k_e$), and $-k_e$ when elimination is slower ($k_e<k_a$). Figure 3b compares the observed extravascular and intravenous terminal windows. A longer extravascular half-life warrants investigating slow absorption, but a finite sampling window can also produce this difference when absorption is faster.

**How to sort a profile.** Read the route record first, then the early samples. Appendix A (separate file) shows simulated reference profiles and published examples for every pattern and route. Consult it for what a pattern looks like, never as evidence about a particular drug.

- **Drug is quantified at the first sample, the rise slows steadily and concentration/time never rises.** Compare first-order, zero-order and parallel inputs, Weibull shape at most 1, a finite window and capacity-limited uptake. A corner at the peak, a near-linear rise, a peak that moves later with dose or a slightly shallower late decline separates them.
- **Early concentrations are near zero, the rise is S-shaped and concentration/time rises.** Compare a fixed lag with transit, Weibull shape above 1, inverse Gaussian and the other gradual inputs. An abrupt start after input-free samples favors a lag. Separating the gradual inputs needs several samples during the rise, and closely spaced early samples in the same animal separate a gradual start from averaging across animals with different lags.
- **A trough and second rise in one animal, or an upward bend after the start.** Compare staggered, sequential or time-dependent input with recirculation. Confirm the feature within an animal, because different individual peak times can give two peaks in a mean, then check dose and meal timing, release evidence and any second rise after IV dosing.
- **A late, broad peak or a decline shallower than the intravenous decline.** Use the flip-flop comparison (question 5) and the route table below.
- **The profile changes with dose or study condition.** Questions 7 and 9 separate a change in input speed from a change in systemic availability or clearance; intravenous data at the same doses help identify clearance changes.

### How does the route change the candidates?

Formulation release, delivery to the absorption site and entry of parent drug into the circulation can run on different time scales. Choose a more detailed model only when the study can constrain the process it adds. The same plasma shape can arise from several routes, so read the route record before the shape.

| Route | What it looks like | Models to compare | Measurements that distinguish them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Oral: gastric emptying, food, regional uptake** | Usually one peak. Food or variable gastric emptying can delay the rise, flatten the peak or split it in two. An absorption window stops uptake early and leaves a corner at the peak. | A single depot (first-order, lag or transit) against intermittent gastric emptying, an absorptive window, or a physiological model of gastric emptying, regional transit, dissolution and permeability (compartmental absorption and transit, convection–dispersion). | Meal and dose times, gastrointestinal imaging or regional delivery, dissolution and permeability data. Oral systemic availability also includes intestinal and hepatic first-pass loss, which an IV reference helps size. |
| **Buccal or sublingual** | A faster rise and earlier peak than oral dosing, then a small late component from the swallowed fraction. Total exposure is below that of a fully absorbed dose. | Direct mucosal uptake against mucosal uptake plus a swallowed fraction with its own timing and first-pass loss. | Administration technique, time in the mouth, a swallowed-dose reference and early serial samples. If the split is poorly identified, report aggregate exposure. |
| **Subcutaneous or intramuscular** | An early peak from a burst or fast fraction, then a decline set by slow release: one long apparent half-life across the late windows, longer than the IV half-life. Without an IV arm it looks like slow disposition. | A fast fraction plus a sustained or delayed depot against a release-limited single depot. For subcutaneous proteins, a local depot with lymphatic transport and local loss. Both routes bypass initial hepatic first pass. | Early and late samples, release or injection-site data, an IV reference for Figure 3b and, where available, lymph measurements. |
| **Inhaled or intratracheal** | An early lung peak, then a broad shoulder or later peak from the swallowed fraction, which enters after a delay with lower availability. The terminal slope is unchanged. If the lung peak falls between samples, the profile resembles one slow oral input. | Pulmonary uptake alone against pulmonary plus swallowed input. Add regional deposition, dissolution and mucociliary clearance when their effects can be checked. | Nominal, emitted or deposited dose, a matched oral or IV reference and a deposition measure. Charcoal given with the dose blocks the swallowed fraction. Plasma alone may leave the fractions unresolved. |
| **Transdermal patch or finite delivery** | A slow rise that can continue until removal, with concentration/time rising for hours. After removal the decline is slower than elimination because drug keeps leaving the skin. | A known delivery schedule against a skin depot or diffusion through skin layers. For pumps and timed injections, drug retained at the site can keep entering after delivery stops. | Application and removal times, delivered dose, release, flux or residual content, and the decline after removal against the IV half-life. Keep the original dosing clock. Apply question 4 only when systemic input from dosing meets its assumptions. |
| **Intraperitoneal** | Like a fast oral input with an early peak. Exposure can fall below the IV value when drug passes the liver first. The terminal slope matches the IV slope, so it can be mistaken for an IV bolus with a late first sample. | Portal uptake with hepatic first pass, for compounds and doses with evidence of portal delivery, against direct systemic uptake. | Route-specific parent exposure against IV, direct portal measurements, and the evidence for the pathway in the species studied. |
| **Second rise after oral or IV dosing** | A trough, then a second peak, often near a meal or gallbladder emptying. The terminal slope is unchanged and exposure is higher than without recycling. A second rise after IV dosing points to recycling rather than the formulation. | A delayed fraction of the dose against biliary excretion with intestinal reabsorption. Record recycled drug separately from first entry when defining $FD$ and $G(t)$. | A serial IV profile, bile or conjugate measurements, and the timing of meals or gallbladder emptying. A trough alone leaves both explanations open. |

## What can we tell from the figures?

Start with questions 1–4 for measured extravascular profiles. For questions 5 and 6, use a qualified comparator when one is available. Question 8 requires comparable IV observations with verified input. Use questions 7 and 9 when the study includes a dose or condition contrast. Use Figure 5 and Table 3 for studies reporting parameters alone.

**1. By when had drug appeared, and did sampling capture the peak?** In Figure 1a, the first quantified sample places an upper bound on the time of systemic appearance when dosing time is reliable, the animal was drug-free at dosing and the assay is specific. An earlier below-limit sample could already contain drug. Under a supported drug-free baseline, shade dosing to first quantification. A positive lower bound on a fixed lag additionally requires a genuinely input-free interval, a concave subsequent concentration rise and concentration and time bounds that support the calculation. Give the observed peak concentration, its time or flat-top interval and the number of quantified samples beforehand. Fewer than two such samples leave the absorption rate poorly informed.

**2. Did the same animal’s concentration fall and rise again?** In Figure 1b, examine every ordered triple of samples after a single dose in each serially sampled animal with a supported drug-free baseline. A trough is resolved if the middle upper concentration bound falls below both outer lower bounds. For each triple take the smaller supported drop or rise. Mark the triple with the largest such value and report both changes. Count the animals sampled on both sides of the feature and how many show it. Different individual peak times or changing contributors can create a second peak in a group mean. Record whether the contributing animals and below-limit handling stay consistent. A mean with at least two post-peak points can describe the group. Destructive sampling cannot establish a second rise within an individual.

A trough that survives the uncertainty and omission checks challenges a single-peaked prediction. This applies to one log-concave input rate on a continuous interval, central input and observation, and a linear, time-invariant disposition response that is itself unimodal. The class includes standard first-order or transit input and a single Weibull input with shape parameter at least 1 under those assumptions. Separate releases and recirculation remain possible explanations, even if the intravenous arm shows no second rise.

**3. How much does the apparent half-life depend on the samples used?** In Figure 1b, fit log concentration against actual time. Hold the last quantified sample fixed and move the start back one eligible observation at a time. Each window needs at least three consecutive eligible quantified samples strictly after the observed peak, a negative slope and the prespecified minimum time span. In this project the configured minimum is two apparent half-lives, calculated for each window. Show gaps and below-limit samples. With slope $b<0$, the apparent half-life is $\ln(2)/(-b)$.

Compare the adjusted coefficient of determination (adjusted R²) across eligible windows. Choose the longest whose adjusted R² is within 0.0001 of the highest value. Phoenix WinNonlin documents the near-tie preference for a fit with more points. The minimum span is a project criterion. Show the fitted segment and, if several windows qualify, give the range of half-lives. The sensitivity inset (optional) can show which starting sample changes the estimate. Dashed lines (optional) describe early and late post-peak slopes. If no window qualifies, leave the apparent half-life unestimated.

**4. Is the early rise compatible with input that starts immediately and never speeds up?** Under that model, concentration divided by time can only stay constant or fall. This prediction assumes a single dose, a drug-free starting state, immediate non-increasing, nonnegative input into the central compartment, and linear, time-invariant disposition with reversible central–peripheral exchange and central parent-drug measurement. In Figure 2, divide the lower concentration bound by the latest plausible time and the upper bound by the earliest plausible time. Use only times whose entire plausible range is positive. Scan every definitely ordered pair in the prespecified full window, including pairs after the peak. Report the largest later lower bound minus earlier upper bound, its units, the two times and the number of pairs.

A positive difference that survives the checks challenges immediate first-order input, finite zero-order input from dosing and no-lag Weibull input with shape at most 1 under these assumptions. For a group curve, check that the animals and weights stay fixed, or justify a common target population, consistent handling of below-limit values and bounds that include sampling uncertainty. If the composition or censoring is unclear, describe the group profile. With at least two quantified samples before the peak, the optional inset shows each concentration change divided by the elapsed time. A fixed lag, transit or gamma delay, an accelerating Weibull rate and release into a depot can give similar early rises. Compare their predictions using the same disposition, residual-error model and handling of below-limit samples.

**5. Does the terminal decline suggest flip-flop kinetics?** Figure 3b compares apparent half-lives across comparable extravascular and intravenous windows. Use the fitted extravascular windows from question 3 and the IV candidate interval from Distribution question 3. Reuse a fitted IV result when its observations and terminal criteria agree with the Absorption comparison. Divide the extravascular half-life by the intravenous half-life. Report the window pair and repeat for other credible pairs. The project describes ratios below 0.80 as steeper after extravascular dosing, 0.80 to below 1.25 as approximately parallel, 1.25 to below 2 as moderately shallower, and 2 or more as markedly shallower. If the category changes with window choice, give the range and leave the directional reading unresolved.

Parallel declines are compatible with a common terminal process. A shallower extravascular decline warrants comparing continuing absorption with other explanations, including different dose-dependent disposition or an early fitting window. A steeper decline warrants checking the selected intervals, distribution phase, arm differences and data quality. Where the intravenous profile has several phases, slow absorption can mask a fast distribution phase in the extravascular profile.

A single extravascular curve can support multiple rate assignments. A late apparent slope can still reflect a mixture of phases if sampling ends too early. A well-supported same-study comparison can prioritize a slow-absorption model. Without that curve, an extravascular half-life at least 1.25 times a comparable terminal value from another extravascular route or a reported intravenous reference provides indirect evidence only. Check the analyte, population, terminal phase and estimation method. With no usable comparator or qualifying windows, the cause remains unresolved. Joint intravenous and extravascular modeling or independent kinetic data can distinguish candidates when the design identifies the relevant rates.

**6. How much parent drug reached the circulation?** In Figure 3a and Table 2, divide each route’s area under the curve by dose, then divide the extravascular result by the intravenous result over the same interval. This estimates absolute systemic bioavailability when total exposure is adequately captured, the parent drug, matrix and dose bases are comparable, and linear clearance is shared. With extravascular arms alone, comparable complete exposures can estimate relative bioavailability under shared linear clearance. Oral systemic bioavailability also reflects intestinal and hepatic first-pass loss.

Missing early intravenous bolus exposure can inflate the ratio when it reduces the IV denominator. Check early coverage in both arms before assigning a direction to the net bias. Document its magnitude or justify an upper bound on the missing share. For an infusion, examine exposure during delivery. At the common endpoint, estimate the remaining area as endpoint concentration divided by the qualified terminal rate when that rate applies there. Divide the remaining area by observed plus remaining area for each route. With a same-study comparison, justified early coverage and an estimated tail fraction of 5% or less for each route, report a **window-based estimate**. The tail estimate depends on the fitted slope. Where coverage or bias direction remains uncertain, report the observed window ratio. A directional bound requires justified directions for all material errors. Retain results above 100% with their uncertainty and review dose basis and intravenous exposure.

**7. Do the curves scale with dose?** In Figure 3, locate separation in the dose-normalized extravascular curves. For a dose series, use an early view extending to about 1.5 times the latest observed peak time and a semilog view of the full profile. In Figure 4a, read the log ratio of higher-dose to reference-dose normalized concentrations at prespecified shared times. A constant offset suggests scaling. A changing ratio suggests a shape difference. A lone matched time describes that time. Strict proportionality predicts zero. Show pointwise 95% confidence intervals when the design supports them. Mark the times that differ after Holm adjustment across the prespecified family.

In Figure 5 compare individual peak time, peak concentration per dose, apparent half-life and common-window area per dose. For the prespecified exposure contrast, report the high-to-reference ratio of dose-normalized areas and a design-appropriate 95% confidence interval. Strict proportionality predicts 1. An interval containing 1 leaves strict proportionality open. For an equivalence claim the entire interval must lie within the prespecified acceptance range. If either the area or matched-time result can trigger a single confirmatory conclusion, prespecify error control across both procedures. Differences can arise from input, systemic bioavailability or clearance.

**8. Can the two routes share one-compartment disposition?** Figure 4b uses the raw extravascular/intravenous concentration ratio at matched times on the same clock. After an intravenous bolus or verified end of infusion, this ratio can only stay constant or rise under shared, linear, time-invariant one-compartment disposition, zero initial drug, central measurement and nonnegative extravascular input. The lower bound on each intravenous denominator must be positive. Compare each earlier ratio’s lower bound with each later ratio’s upper bound and mark the largest supported decrease. For group means, justify a common elimination rate or an appropriate population argument under a consistent target and censoring rule. Use supported uncertainty for the whole contrast, including the pairing where applicable. An unknown intravenous input history or visibly multiexponential intravenous decline precludes this formal check. It can run without a qualified terminal fit and answers a different question from the half-life comparison in Figure 3b.

**9. Could one multiplier explain the difference between conditions?** Name the reference, dose basis and every condition that differs, including formulation, feeding and occasion. When questions 7 and 9 use the same arms, times and basis, reuse the ratio values and uncertainty. Question 7 tests equality after dose normalization. This question asks whether any one positive scale factor fits all times. In Figure 4c, determine which positive multipliers can map reference concentrations to test concentrations at all matched times within their bounds. Use the original concentration intervals when a denominator can be zero. Check the timing, population and, for a group mean, how individual scales relate to individual profiles. When the two arms each have at least three quantified samples in the common window, an empty intersection that survives the uncertainty and omission checks challenges a change in bioavailability alone under shared input shape and disposition. It also challenges proportional changes in clearances and volumes that preserve rate constants, alone or combined with a change in bioavailability.

To compare shape visually, divide each curve by its positive exposure over the same window (Figure 3a inset). To test whether *only input speed* changed, compare complete dose-normalized exposures. They should agree with identical systemic fraction, the same effective input site and shared linear disposition. Bound missing areas in both arms before using this prediction. For repeated equal doses at matched intervals, a supported decrease in exposure relative to the first drug-free interval challenges identical input per dose and time-invariant linear disposition. A single-formulation study cannot resolve a between-formulation question.

## Sanity checks and outlier handling

| What the reviewer sees | What to check or report |
| --- | --- |
| **Unknown route, dosing history or measured signal** | Trace source, animal, dose, units, actual administration and draw times, occasion, formulation, analyte and matrix. With an unknown route, defer absorption interpretation. With an assay combining endogenous and administered drug, show the observations and examine baseline. After inhalation or intratracheal dosing, keep both pulmonary and swallowed contributions in view unless independent evidence bounds enteral uptake. |
| **Predose signal or a sample beyond an assay limit** | Investigate quantified predose drug on a documented first dose. After prior dosing, a predose value at or below 5% of the observed peak counts as negligible only for peak and exposure interpretation under the project rule. Quantified residual drug leaves onset unresolved. Check the tail, common endpoint and any depot separately. All samples below the quantification limit leave absorption unassessable. A peak above the upper quantification limit gives a lower bound on peak concentration. Propagate the bound through each subsequent calculation. |
| **Gaps, duplicates or incomplete exposure** | Resolve duplicate times against the source. Identify technical replicates and retain unresolved conflicts. Keep censoring limits. Use common actual endpoints and comparable grids or quantify integration error. Use linear trapezoids on rising or flat segments and logarithmic trapezoids on positive falling segments. End integration at the last quantified sample before terminal below-limit values. An interior below-limit sample requires a prespecified censoring treatment for an estimate of the entire area or its confidence interval. |
| **An unexpected point or an apparent second rise** | Check sample identity, another dose, actual times, assay flags and digitization against the source. Keep a plausible unusual value in the primary analysis. Correct a verified transcription error from its source. Exclude a confirmed invalid measurement only from the affected calculation. Record the original value, animal, time, source, reason and effect on the conclusion. |
| **A nominal difference looks larger than the error bars** | Identify assay, between-animal, timing and digitization uncertainty for the analysis unit. A search across pairs or triples needs justified bounds for all observations, simultaneous statistical coverage, or a calibrated global procedure covering the search. A matched source assay coefficient of variation converted to joint bounds over every searched observation (Bonferroni), or replicate-based bounds with stated coverage, count as justified joint bounds. Report its error level where applicable. An assay acceptance criterion, unconverted assay precision, separate pointwise intervals or an assumed project tolerance alone cannot support formal exclusion. With assumed error, report the margin, its direction and decisive samples as a sensitivity result. |
| **The same animals contributed more than one measurement** | Calculate exposure per animal for serial data. Pair crossover records within animal and account for period and carryover as applicable. Separate arm summaries do not recover within-animal covariance. For independent groups, compatible summaries of individual exposures, with between-animal variability and sample sizes, may support inference. Time-specific concentration variability alone does not recover individual area variability. With destructive sampling, use an estimator of uncertainty that matches the integration method and design. |

**Does the result depend on one observation?** Keep the primary result. For each formal check, remove every eligible observation in turn and repeat the full calculation on the rest. Rescan all concentration/time pairs or within-animal trough triples. For the route comparison remove the matched time from both arms. For conditions, recompute the intersection of allowable multipliers. For dose comparisons, recalculate the exposure contrast and interval or repeat the Holm procedure over the remaining times. Identify the removal unit and retain the original window definition. Dropping a required endpoint can make the original contrast unassessable.

Report whether the contradiction persisted, disappeared in an assessable rerun, or could no longer be tested. Only the second outcome shows that the assessable finding depended on the omitted unit. Removing an animal can also widen an interval through loss of precision. Withhold a formal exclusion if required independent support cannot be assessed, or if a prespecified uncertainty widening changes the decision.

## Answer the main question

Give the timing, extent and model conclusion separately. Lead with an observation the reader can locate. For each arm, name the first quantified sample, how well the peak was sampled, the animal or cohort, and the relevant uncertainty. Say which complete input–disposition combination the data contradict, which alternatives warrant fitting, and what measurement could distinguish them. When available, report the half-life comparison, matched route-ratio check and exposure estimate separately.

| Conclusion | What to report |
| --- | --- |
| **The observations contradict a model under the stated assumptions** | Name the full input, disposition, initial state and measured signal, the animal or group, the window, the decisive samples, the margin or interval and its uncertainty basis. Apply the conclusion only to combinations covered by that prediction. |
| **The model remains possible** | Say whether the data were compatible throughout or whether widening the bounds or omitting a unit changed the decision. Identify the check and its effect. |
| **The study cannot decide** | Name the missing comparison, assumption or uncertainty estimate. Give the direction and size of any sensitivity result and retain the model. |

**Ranked hypotheses for Modeling.** For each study and species, rank the viable hypotheses from strongest to weakest support. Give each hypothesis with its supporting and opposing observations, figure or source record, analysis unit, time window, uncertainty and the next observation or fit that could distinguish it. Treat estimates derived from the same measurements as one source of evidence. Tie candidates when the data cannot distinguish them and use model simplicity to choose which tied candidate to fit first. Keep candidates with only indirect support, untested candidates and candidates with contrary evidence in that order. Record formally excluded combinations with their assumptions and evidence separately from the viable list. A contradiction supported only at sensitivity level moves a candidate below the other candidates with the same level of support, ahead of the next level; it does not exclude the candidate.

**Models to compare in this module.** Retain each prespecified option across onset, input shape, second input, terminal phase, dose dependence and alternative disposition. Use the simplest immediate first-order, single-input, dose-linear, disposition-controlled combination as the reference fit. If formally contradicted, keep it as a comparison benchmark and record its exclusion from the viable set. Among the remaining candidates, first compare those supported by a reproducible individual feature or a relevant comparison with justified uncertainty. For two input waves, compare a delayed second fraction with the best single-input fit on the same records. Check whether both fractions and their timing are estimable across starting values. When a fitted fraction approaches zero, its timing can become unidentifiable. A routine chi-square likelihood-ratio test may then be miscalibrated. If the fits predict the same observations within uncertainty, report which parameters remain unresolved and carry the simpler fit forward. A source-supported recirculation model can be compared alongside them.

If serial profiles differ across animals, compare continuous variation in lag or rate with an explicit population mixture only when the individual records can distinguish the patterns. In a population mixture, different animals can follow different input models. A parallel-input model sends two fractions through the same animal. With repeated occasions, examine whether the pattern repeats within each animal.

Name the study feature or measurement that warrants each additional candidate. For flexible input, compare predictions across smoothing choices and, where available, in animals or occasions held out from estimation. Preserve within-animal dependence when evaluating uncertainty or predictions.

## When other evidence adds to the answer

**What did the assay measure?** For absolute parent bioavailability, use the same parent analyte and intravenous administration of that parent. After a prodrug, an intravenous reference for the active drug can support an estimate of active-moiety availability under shared linear active-drug clearance and a stoichiometrically matched molar dose basis. A metabolite exposure ratio alone leaves parent bioavailability unresolved. If the assay combines endogenous and administered drug, show the measured profile and review baseline before assigning any signal to the dose.

**Dose and repeat-dose evidence.** A saturable input rate with unchanged extent can lower peak concentration per dose and delay the peak while preserving complete exposure per dose under shared linear clearance. Falling complete exposure per dose can reflect lower systemic bioavailability or higher clearance. Rising exposure per dose can reflect higher systemic bioavailability or saturable elimination. As a descriptive modeling comparison, allow relative bioavailability or the absorption rate constant to vary by dose, one at a time. Compare the result with a capacity-limited uptake model when site-specific evidence exists. The dose trend alone cannot distinguish absorption from a change in clearance.

For repeat dosing, verify the route, formulation, dose, interval and dosing history. Interpret changes across routes or formulations as regimen contrasts. Under shared time-invariant linear disposition and identical input per dose, a later interval can contain residual exposure from earlier doses. At steady state, exposure over one interval equals complete first-dose exposure at the same dose. Compare single-dose and repeated-dose windows on that basis.

**Studies reporting parameters alone.** Use Table 3 for isolated values and Figure 5 for a comparable series. Give the source definition, method, units, window, sample size and reported variability. For reported bioavailability, give both route doses and the identity of the intravenous reference. A reported half-life can support an indirect terminal comparison if the source defines its phase and window. A half-life alone supplies no exposure denominator. Keep absorption rate, lag, systemic bioavailability and mean absorption time specific to the route, formulation and species. Carry unresolved disposition, elimination or metabolism questions to the relevant analysis.
